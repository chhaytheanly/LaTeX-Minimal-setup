\label{ch:methodology}

In this chapter, we present the step-by-step methodologies, model architectures, and edge deployment pipelines employed in the development of a real-time, high-precision visual inspection system for tallying metallic fasteners on industrial conveyor belts.\\

Rather than presenting the final proposed model in isolation, this chapter systematically details the iterative research, design, benchmarking, and engineering lifecycle. The methodology is structured around four consecutive phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Collection and Preprocessing:}
    Establishing a purpose-built industrial fastener dataset consisting of
    gears, bearings, and nuts, followed by geometric data augmentation and
    image enhancement using Histogram Equalization
    \cite{yaseen2024yolov8indepthexplorationinternal,
    khanam2024yolov11overviewkeyarchitectural,
    jocher2026ultralyticsyolo26unifiedrealtime}.
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 -- High-Accuracy Target Modeling:} Training a Transformer-based Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) to establish a maximum theoretical accuracy baseline, aligning directly with client-specific precision objectives~\cite{zhao2024detrsbeatyolosrealtime}.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 -- Edge Baseline Benchmarking:} Evaluating standard convolutional deep learning models including YOLOv8, YOLOv11, and YOLOv10/v26 across different scale sizes (nano, small, medium) to analyze the speed-accuracy-latency trade-offs.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3 -- Proposed Model Implementation:} Synthesizing these empirical trade-offs to design and train our custom \textbf{YOLO11-GED} model (in nano, small, and medium scale variants). This architecture strategically integrates the novel \textbf{EMAc2f} attention module, \textbf{GhostBottleneck} efficiency, \textbf{DySample} dynamic upsampling \cite{zhao2025dyglnethybridgloballocalfeature,han2020ghostnetfeaturescheapoperations,zhao2025dyglnethybridgloballocalfeature}.
\end{enumerate}

The subsequent sections detail each phase, followed by an in-depth breakdown of the modular Cham-Rorb software runtime deployed on the NVIDIA Jetson edge platform.

\section{System Architecture Overview}
\label{sec:system_arch}

To ensure the scalability, real-time processing, and seamless integration of the proposed edge-AI framework, the entire project structure is built upon the \textbf{Separation of Concerns (SoC)} principle~\cite{daga2006soc}. By decoupling the computationally intensive machine learning pipeline from the data persistence and user interface layers, the system achieves high modularity and maintainability. The high-level architecture, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}, is divided into four primary subsystems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vision Engine (Python):} The core inference pipeline. It handles camera ingestion, executes the YOLO11-GED model, applies ByteTrack for temporal consistency, and performs geometric counting (line, zone, polygon). Events are published via WebSockets.
    \item \textbf{Backend API (TypeScript/Bun):} A high-throughput asynchronous server that routes real-time telemetry from the Vision Engine to clients and persists historical logs via a Prisma ORM database.
    \item \textbf{Interactive UI (React/Vite):} A lightweight web dashboard for remote monitoring of live streams and analytics.
    \item \textbf{Desktop Application (PyQt6):} A native client for on-site operators requiring low-latency, high-bitrate local video rendering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preprocessing and Augmentation}
Metallic surfaces are highly susceptible to specular highlights and uneven lighting. To mitigate this, \textbf{Histogram Equalization (HE)} was applied as a preprocessing step to enhance local contrast and normalize illumination across the dataset~\cite{430, 434, 441}. 

During training, the following geometric and photometric augmentations were employed to prevent overfitting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mosaic and MixUp:} To improve the model's ability to detect small, densely packed fasteners by forcing the network to learn contextual cues from multiple overlapping images.
    \item \textbf{Random Affine Transformations:} Including rotation ($\pm 15^\circ$), scaling, and translation to simulate varying camera angles and fastener orientations.
\end{itemize}

\section{High-Accuracy Baseline with RT-DETR}
\label{sec:phase2_rtdetr}

Before optimizing for edge deployment constraints, it is essential to establish the theoretical upper bound of detection accuracy on the proposed dataset. To this end, the \textbf{Real-Time DEtection TRansformer (RT-DETR)}~\cite{zhao2024detrsbeatyolosrealtime} was selected as the Phase II baseline.

Unlike CNN-based detectors, RT-DETR leverages a transformer encoder-decoder architecture with an efficient hybrid encoder. The self-attention mechanism captures long-range dependencies, enabling the model to resolve heavily occluded fasteners by leveraging global context. Furthermore, its decoder-based detection head eliminates the need for hand-crafted anchor boxes and Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) post-processing bottlenecks.

Training RT-DETR on the fastener dataset yielded an accuracy ceiling of 99\% mAP@0.5:0.982. However, its inference speed of around 28 up FPS on the target NVIDIA Jetson hardware rendered it unsuitable for the client's real-time conveyor requirements (which demand $>30$ FPS). This finding motivated the transition to Phase III, where lightweight CNN-based detectors were evaluated.

\label{sec:phase4_custom}

Building upon the empirical insights from Phases II and III, the \textbf{YOLO11-GED} architecture was designed as a purpose-built solution. The name reflects its core innovations: \textbf{G}hostBottleneck efficiency, \textbf{E}MA attention, and \textbf{D}ySample upsampling.

The medium variant's lower \texttt{max\_channels} cap (768 vs.\ 1024) intentionally throttles the deepest layers to preserve real-time throughput on the Jetson platform while retaining sufficient representational capacity for the fastener domain.

\subsection{Strategic Module Placement}
A key contribution of this work is the \textit{surgical placement} of custom modules. Applying heavy attention mechanisms to shallow layers (P1--P3) yields diminishing returns, as these layers primarily capture low-level edges. Therefore, YOLO11-GED employs a hybrid backbone:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{P1--P3 (Shallow):} Retain standard \texttt{Conv} + \texttt{C2f} blocks for efficient, low-level feature extraction.
    \item \textbf{P4--P5 (Deep):} Deploy \texttt{EMAc2f} blocks. At these deeper stages, feature maps possess high-level semantic context, making them the optimal location for attention to resolve occlusions.
    \item \textbf{Neck (P5 $\rightarrow$ P4):} Deploys \texttt{DySample} to prevent semantic blurring during the most critical high-to-medium resolution fusion.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{GhostBottleneck Formulation}
Standard convolutions compute all output feature maps independently, which is computationally redundant. The GhostBottleneck~\cite{han2020ghostnetfeaturescheapoperations} mitigates this by splitting the operation into intrinsic feature generation and cheap linear "ghost" feature synthesis. Given an input tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{in} \times H \times W}$, the output $Y_{ghost}$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{intrinsic} = \text{Conv}_{1 \times 1}(X)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Y_{ghost} = \text{Linear}_{1 \times 1}(\text{DWConv}_{k \times k}(Y_{intrinsic}))
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Y_{out} = \text{Concat}(Y_{intrinsic}, Y_{ghost}) + \text{Shortcut}(X)
\end{equation}
where $\text{DWConv}$ denotes a depthwise convolution. This reduces the computational complexity from $\mathcal{O}(C_{in} C_{out} k^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(C_{in} C' k^2 + C' C_{out})$, where $C' \approx C_{out}/2$, preserving representational power while halving the FLOPs.

\subsubsection{Efficient Multi-Scale Attention (EMA)}
The EMA module~\cite{Ouyang_2023} is applied as a residual branch to the \texttt{EMAc2f} block. Unlike standard attention that reduces channel dimensions (causing information loss), EMA preserves full channel capacity by operating on grouped features. Let the input be $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times H \times W}$, divided into $G$ groups, yielding $X_g \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot G) \times C_g \times H \times W}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/EMAc2f.png}
    \caption{Attention maps generated by the EMA module to focus on fastener features.}
    \label{fig:ema}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Step 1: Cross-Dimensional Spatial Pooling.} 
To capture long-range spatial dependencies without global flattening, 1D global pooling is applied along the height ($H$) and width ($W$) dimensions:
\begin{equation}
    X_H = \text{Pool}_H(X_g) \in \mathbb{R}^{C_g \times H \times 1}, \quad X_W = \text{Pool}_W(X_g) \in \mathbb{R}^{C_g \times 1 \times W}
\end{equation}
These are concatenated along the spatial dimension and fused via a $1 \times 1$ convolution to model cross-dimensional interactions, then split and passed through a sigmoid activation $\sigma(\cdot)$ to generate directional gating weights $M_H$ and $M_W$.

\textbf{Step 2: Dual-Branch Feature Refinement.}
The original grouped features are refined through two parallel branches:
\begin{align}
    F_1 &= \text{GroupNorm}\left(X_g \odot \sigma(M_H) \odot \sigma(M_W)\right) \\
    F_2 &= \text{Conv}_{3 \times 3}(X_g)
\end{align}
where $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product. $F_1$ represents spatially gated features, while $F_2$ captures local neighborhood context.

\textbf{Step 3: Channel-Spatial Matrix Aggregation.}
To dynamically emphasize fine-grained textures of overlapping metallic fasteners, EMA computes a spatial attention map by multiplying channel-wise importance with spatial feature maps. Let $\text{GAP}(\cdot)$ be Global Average Pooling and $\text{Flat}(\cdot)$ flatten the spatial dimensions to $H \times W$:
\begin{align}
    V_1 &= \text{Softmax}(\text{GAP}(F_1)) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times C_g} \\
    M_2 &= \text{Flat}(F_2) \in \mathbb{R}^{C_g \times (H \cdot W)} \\
    A_{spatial} &= \sigma\left( \text{Reshape}\Big( V_1 M_2 + V_2 M_1 \Big) \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times H \times W}
\end{align}
Here, $V_1 M_2$ computes a weighted sum of spatial features in $F_2$ based on the channel importance of $F_1$. The final EMA output is reconstructed to the original batch and channel dimensions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{EMA}(X) = \text{Reshape}_{B, C}\Big( X_g \odot A_{spatial} \Big)
\end{equation}
The final block output is $Y = X_{out} + \text{EMA}(X_{out})$, ensuring stable gradient flow.

\subsubsection{DySample: Dynamic Content-Aware Upsampling}
In the neck's top-down pathway (P5 $\rightarrow$ P4), standard nearest-neighbor or bilinear upsampling blurs high-frequency edges critical for fastener detection, while Deformable Convolutions (DCN) are too computationally heavy. We employ DySample~\cite{zhao2025dyglnethybridgloballocalfeature}, a lightweight dynamic sampling module.

Given a feature map $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ and an upsampling scale factor $s$, the target resolution is $H' = sH, W' = sW$. The process is defined in three steps:\\

\textbf{1. Offset Prediction:} A lightweight $1 \times 1$ convolution predicts spatial offsets, initialized to zero to ensure the module behaves as standard bilinear upsampling at the start of training:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta P_{raw} = \text{Conv}_{1 \times 1}(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{2s^2 \times H \times W}
\end{equation}

\textbf{2. Grid Generation and Bounding:} The offsets are rearranged via pixel shuffle to match the target resolution, yielding $\Delta P \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times H' \times W'}$. A normalized base sampling grid $G_{base} \in [-1, 1]^{H' \times W' \times 2}$ is generated. To prevent extreme spatial distortion and ensure training stability, the offsets are bounded using a $\tanh$ activation and scaled by a factor $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
    G_{dyn} = G_{base} + \tanh(\Delta P) \cdot \lambda, \quad \text{where } \lambda = \frac{2.0}{\max(H, W)}
\end{equation}
The scaling factor $\lambda$ is critical: it converts pixel-level offsets into the normalized $[-1, 1]$ coordinate space required by the grid sampler, preventing gradient explosion.\\

\textbf{3. Dynamic Bilinear Sampling:} The final upsampled feature map $Y$ is obtained by sampling $X$ at the dynamically perturbed coordinates $G_{\mathrm{dyn}}$:
\begin{equation}
    Y(c, i, j) = \sum_{m,n} X(c, i_m, j_n) \cdot \max(0, 1 - |i - i_m|) \cdot \max(0, 1 - |j - j_n|)
\end{equation}
where $(i_m, j_n)$ are the continuous coordinates derived from $G_{\mathrm{dyn}}(i, j)$. This allows the network to adaptively ``look around'' adjacent pixels based on local content, sharpening upsampled features of small, densely packed fasteners at a fraction of the computational cost of DCN.

\section{Edge Deployment and Tracking Pipeline}
\label{sec:deployment}

Deploying the trained models onto the NVIDIA Jetson edge platform requires bridging the gap between Python-based training and C++-based inference. 

\subsection{TensorRT Acceleration}
The PyTorch models are first exported to the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format. Subsequently, the NVIDIA TensorRT SDK is utilized to optimize the ONNX graph. TensorRT applies layer fusion, kernel auto-tuning, and precision calibration (FP16 or INT8) to maximize throughput on the Jetson's CUDA cores and Tensor cores.

\subsection{Temporal Consistency via ByteTrack}
A critical limitation of frame-wise detection on fast-moving conveyors is prediction oscillation and double-counting due to transient occlusions. To resolve this, the Vision Engine integrates the \textbf{ByteTrack} algorithm~\cite{zhang2022bytetrackmultiobjecttrackingassociating}

Unlike traditional trackers that discard low-confidence detections, ByteTrack utilizes both high and low-confidence bounding boxes. High-confidence boxes initiate new tracks, while low-confidence boxes are associated with existing tracks based on Intersection over Union (IoU) similarity. This ensures that a fastener temporarily obscured by a glare or overlapping part maintains its unique Track ID, preventing the counting logic from registering it as a new object.

\subsection{Geometric Counting Logic}
Once stabilized Track IDs are established, the Counter module evaluates the trajectory centroids against user-defined spatial constraints (Lines, Zones, or Polygons). A fastener is tallied only when its tracked centroid crosses the defined geometric boundary in the correct directional vector, ensuring absolute counting accuracy regardless of conveyor speed or camera angle.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

To comprehensively evaluate the proposed framework, metrics are divided into accuracy and efficiency categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Average Precision (mAP):} mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 are used to evaluate localization and classification accuracy across varying IoU thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Precision and Recall:} To measure the rate of false positives (background noise detected as fasteners) and false negatives (missed fasteners).
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency and FPS:} Measured in milliseconds (ms) and Frames Per Second (FPS) directly on the target Jetson hardware using TensorRT, excluding pre/post-processing overhead.
    \item \textbf{Model Complexity:} Total Parameter count (M) and Floating Point Operations per Second (FLOPs) to quantify the computational footprint.
\end{itemize}
